Check first, then test a simple follow-up service for patients with repeated hospital stays. Don't build AI now.

The patient problem

Patients sent home after 2 or more hospital stays in the past year came back into hospital within 30 days 21% of the time, vs 8% for other patients sent home. Fact

Why is unknown: maybe unclear medicines, no follow-up booked, or little help at home. To test

  • Patients: agreed follow-up done, fewer problems after going home To test

Decision we ask for today: approve an 8–12 week check of our own patients, costs and payment rules Assumption: timing. No build. If the checks pass, run a small, fair test against normal care.

Would we build AI for this now?

No. Patients sent home after two or more hospital stays last year came back within 30 days far more often (21% vs 8%), and the gap remained after allowing for age, illness and other factors. But we have not shown what need is unmet, that a service fixes it, or that it pays. Until then there is nothing for AI to improve: a simple, visible rule is enough to choose who gets a needs check. Check the need and the money, test a simple workflow, then compare AI fairly.

US setting. Data: 101,766 US hospital stays with diabetes recorded, 130 hospitals, 1999–2008 (Strack et al., 2014). "Came back into hospital" = readmitted within 30 days as recorded. Care at hospitals outside the same health system is not captured.

Biggest caution

Old data (1999–2008), many hospitals mixed together, no costs, and care outside the same health system is missed. It shows a pattern, not the cause, and not whether these hospital stays could be prevented.

What the data shows

  • Starting point: 99,343 stays (69,990 people) after removing patients who died or went to hospice. 11.4% came back within 30 days. Fact
  • Target vs others sent home: 1,580 of 7,402 stays (21.3%) vs 4,022 of 52,832 (7.6%). Fact
  • Checks: counting each person once: about 18% vs 6% Fact. After allowing for age, illness, stay length, medicines and more: about 18% vs 8% Estimate.
  • Concentrated: 12% of home-discharge stays, but 28% of quick returns (19% counting each person once). Fact

4 · Intervention

A rules-led follow-up run by case managers. The system handles the tasks, and people make the decisions.

1 · FlagAt discharge planning: 2+ stays in the past year and going home.
2 · Needs checkCase manager asks about medicines, help at home, transport and existing services.
3 · Agree a planFollow-up care booked, with timing set by clinical need.
4 · Check inCall or message after going home, timed by need and payer rules.
5 · EscalateProblems routed by urgency to a named coordinator, with an out-of-hours route.

System does / people do

System: flag the record, create and assign tasks, help book visits, send approved reminders, track handovers, chase overdue tasks.

People: judge need, agree the plan with the patient, decide urgency, handle exceptions, sign off care.

Owner: case management, with doctor sign-off. First check what our current record system can already do.

How we will measure it

  • Patient: agreed follow-up done and needs resolved (baseline unknown)
  • Outcome: came back within 30 days (old data: 21%) Fact
  • Money: net income or real saving per patient enrolled
  • Side effects: staff workload, ER visits, patient burden

Compared with: normal discharge care, and the same service offered to all patients sent home.

Why not AI now

There is no proven service yet for AI to target better. A simple, visible rule is enough to choose who gets a needs check, and staff can see why a patient was flagged.

Later, if the service works: test AI fairly, on separate test patients, using only information known at the time of the decision.

Steps and timings are a starting design Assumption, to be set with clinical staff. Whether follow-up can be billed depends on payer rules (for example, contact and visit deadlines), which must be checked.
